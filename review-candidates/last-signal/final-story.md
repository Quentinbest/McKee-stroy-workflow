# The Last Signal

Mara Venn was labeling the dead equipment when the weather printer woke.

The district office had sent orange tags for everything the new offshore beacon
made unnecessary. RADIO. BAROMETER. FRESNEL DRIVE. She wrote each word in block
capitals, the hand she had used for thirty years of storm logs, then looped the
tags through brass handles gone green at their screws.

Salt had bloomed white around the printer's copper ground. Mara scraped it with
her thumbnail, added `GROUND FAULT` to the disposal sheet, and pulled the data
cable.

The printer struck one line through its dry ribbon.

`2056-11-18 0214 KEEP KESTREL LIT 0317 BELLWEATHER`

Bellweather was the island ferry due before four.

Mara opened the printer housing. No battery. No radio board. Only a ribbon,
hammer bank, and the ground wire disappearing into the wall toward the archive
sensor. She lifted the wire from its post.

The paper advanced.

`0219 NORTH SENSOR FAIL`

At 02:19 the north anemometer stopped. Its cups froze against the rain, though
the south instrument still registered forty-one knots.

Mara wrote the result on the paper. A second line predicted a seven-second power
dip at 02:26. She counted against the sweep of the old lens. The gallery went
black at four and returned at eleven.

The Fresnel lens caught her flashlight and multiplied one Mara into twenty
curved panes.

She called Ivo Chen.

"A disconnected printer is issuing forecasts."

"Then it is not disconnected."

"I have the ground wire in my hand."

Keys clicked at his end. "The handover protocol has a fatigue clause."

"I know what fatigue is."

"You have been awake since yesterday."

Mara looked at the retirement folder beside the radio. Ivo had placed a yellow
tab where she was to sign at three, after the offshore beacon assumed sole
authority.

"Send me a photograph," he said. "Do not energize the old lamp."

The printer hammered before she could lift her phone.

`ARCHIVE ERROR 1996-11-18 0312-0324`

For thirty years Mara had trained herself not to hesitate at that date.

She photographed the message. In the image, the fresh letters floated over the
faint carbon ghosts of old forecasts.

Ivo called back. "The timestamp is impossible. The district network has no
route to that machine."

"It predicted the sensor."

"A failed sensor in this weather is not a prediction."

"And the power?"

"Kestrel's voltage regulator is in the replacement order you signed."

He was using her record against the evidence: no mystery, only neglected
equipment she had already documented. His voice softened.

"Mara, no one has maintained a station as long as you have. Finish cleanly."

The 1996 commendation used the same word.

She carried the message into the archive room. Bound logs filled the metal
shelves, black spines for weather, red for distress traffic. The paper for
November 1996 opened by habit to the storm crease.

03:05, visibility one cable.

03:12, blank.

03:24, distress call from harbor launch.

The official ink did not shake. That was what the board had admired.

Tomas had called at 03:12. Mara had sent her younger brother into that storm
after mistaking static for a trawler's distress signal. When his launch flooded,
he asked for a bearing home. She froze with the microphone in her hand. Twelve
minutes later, when the rescue service answered, she entered the call at 03:24.
The false time changed no weather. It changed where the inquiry placed blame.

The clean log preserved her job. The sea kept Tomas.

Behind the shelf, the printer resumed, each blow transmitted through the wall.

`MODEL SOURCE KESTREL ARCHIVE`

Mara removed the service manual from its locked drawer. Inside lay the yellow
carbon sheet she had hidden before the inquiry. Tomas's real call time remained
pressed into it backward, a bruise that could still be read in a mirror.

The future station had learned Bellwether storms from her archive. Its missing
twelve minutes were not an absence to the model. They were a rule.

At 02:51 Bellweather broke into the district channel.

"Kestrel, this is Bellweather. Offshore beacon intermittent. Request harbor
bearing."

Captain Nia Holt kept her voice level, but behind it a child asked whether the
light would come on. Mara pictured the paper lighthouses the island school sold
on the ferry each winter, battery candles glowing in folded windows.

She reached for the microphone.

Ivo answered first. "Bellweather, maintain the certified approach. Kestrel is
decommissioned at zero three hundred."

"We have lost the outer green," Nia said. "Radar is painting rain from the
cliff."

"The inner leading marks remain active."

Mara looked through the gallery glass. The lower leading mark vanished, returned
red, then vanished again behind driven water.

The printer struck so hard the ribbon cover jumped.

`BREAKWATER SUBMERGED`

`KEEP KESTREL LIT 0317`

`CORRECT SOURCE OR BEARING INVALID`

Mara keyed the old lamp test circuit. A red interlock blocked it: DISTRICT KEY.

Ivo arrived at 03:06, rain running from his coat onto the mosaic floor. He held
the brass shutdown key on its steel ring.

"Step away from the panel."

She gave him the future page and the carbon sheet.

He read the two call times. His thumb stopped over 03:12.

"Is this authentic?"

"Yes."

"Why was it not in the inquiry file?"

"Because I removed it."

The wind pressed a low note from the gallery door.

Ivo set his tablet on the desk and opened the handover form. The yellow signature
box waited. "If we suspend the transfer, I have to state a cause."

"State it."

"The cause would include this document."

"Yes."

"Your service finding can be reopened. Benefits can be held pending review."

He did not say pension. He turned the tablet so she could see the clause.

Bellweather called again. Static cut Nia's words into pieces.

"...lower mark gone...eleven aboard...one child..."

A smaller voice took the microphone. "Kestrel, I made your lighthouse."

Nia pulled it away. "Sorry. We are approaching the turn."

The printer gave one incomplete line:

`0317 COURSE`

Then nothing.

Mara had spent thirty years keeping corrosion from circuits, oil in gears,
numbers in their columns. She had called it responsibility because the word
maintenance was too small for a life. Now the station offered her the thing she
had never maintained: the truth on which every later reading rested.

Ivo put the shutdown key into the panel. "I cannot authorize a navigational aid
from an unverified source."

"I am not asking you to."

Mara opened the recorded emergency channel.

"District, Bellweather, this is keeper Mara Venn at Kestrel Head. Correction to
station log, November eighteenth, 1996. Distress call from harbor launch was
received at 03:12, not 03:24. I falsified the entry and withheld its carbon
copy."

Ivo reached for the microphone, perhaps to stop her, perhaps to make the record
precise. Mara could not tell.

She entered the true time at the archive terminal. The checksum changed. On the
weather printer, the unfinished word completed:

`0317 COURSE 284`

Ivo's hand closed around the key. "This still does not prove the lamp is safe."

"No."

"Or the bearing."

"No."

"Then what are you doing?"

"Putting my name on the choice."

She took the key from the panel before he decided whether to resist. The ring
cut her palm. She turned it to MANUAL, opened the drive cabinet, and engaged the
motor.

The gear hummed without moving. Salt had entered the upper bearing.

03:15.

Mara pulled the hand crank from its clips. The orange disposal tag fluttered
from it: FRESNEL DRIVE. She tore the tag free, fitted the crank, and leaned her
weight into the iron.

The gear moved one tooth. Grease blackened her retirement blouse. Ivo braced the
cabinet, not helping the choice, only keeping the housing from walking across
the floor.

03:16.

Mara cranked until the lens began its old measured turn. She threw the lamp
switch.

At 03:17 Kestrel Head opened its eye.

The beam crossed the gallery, struck the rain, and returned as a white wall. It
swept again. Far below, Bellweather's mast light appeared between waves, too far
east of the old approach.

"Kestrel light acquired," Nia said.

"From the light, steer two-eight-four," Mara answered. "Turn now."

The ferry's green starboard light held its course for one terrible sweep, then
slid west. A wave withdrew from the harbor mouth. The breakwater showed beneath
it, black blocks where the automated route had been.

Bellweather passed the outer edge with less than a vessel's length to spare.

No one in the station spoke. The lens turned. Each time it faced the archive
room, the carbon sheet flashed pale in Mara's hand.

At 03:28 the weather printer produced a final line.

`2056-11-18 BELLWEATHER ARRIVED 0402`

`SOURCE CORRECTED`

Ivo photographed the terminal, the carbon copy, the key in Mara's bleeding
hand. He sealed each image into the incident file.

"I have to suspend you," he said.

"I know."

"The inquiry team lands at nine."

Mara watched the beam find Bellweather once more inside the harbor. In a cabin
window, a paper lighthouse blinked back.

At dawn she shut Kestrel down herself.

Three weeks later, pending the inquiry, Mara sat in the district archive with
the young technician assigned to migrate the old logs. They worked from the
original pages, never from summaries. When they reached November 1996, Mara set
the stained carbon copy beside the clean official line.

"Which one do I enter?" the technician asked.

"Both," Mara said. "And the reason they differ."

Outside, the offshore beacon flashed on schedule. It needed no keeper and sent
no message from the future.
